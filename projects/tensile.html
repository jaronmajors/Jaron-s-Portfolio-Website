Braided Body Tensile Pull Testing

Abbott Laboratories — Cardiac Rhythm Management Product Development Engineering Co-op Sylmar, CA

Summary

In a worst-case scenario, the braided body protecting the AVEIR CSP's lead could fracture during chronic use — and if that happens, the extended electrode still needs enough tensile strength to be safely retrieved. I ran testing to quantify exactly how much strength the braided body retains after different levels of damage, to set a hard design input for how much fracture the device could tolerate while still meeting a minimum retrieval pull strength.

What I Did

  • Used the failure modes identified in fatigue bend testing to define realistic fracture geometries at 25% and 50% depth into the braided body.
  • Designed and built pull-test fixturing on an Instron to grip and load damaged samples without introducing artificial stress concentrations.
  • Ran tensile pull testing to failure on 25%-fractured, 50%-fractured, and undamaged control samples.
  • Compared average force-to-failure across the three groups and calculated percent strength reduction relative to the control.
  • Iterated on fixturing and sample prep across several rounds to get clean, repeatable break data.

Conclusions & Results

  • Average force to failure was 14.153 lbs for the control, 11.401 lbs at 25% fracture, and 6.271 lbs at 50% fracture.
  • That's a 19% strength reduction at 25% fracture depth and a 57% reduction at 50% fracture depth, relative to an undamaged sample.
  • The current design input for composite pull strength is 2 lbs — even the 50%-fractured samples cleared that bar with meaningful margin, showing the braided body still holds acceptable retrieval strength even after significant damage.
  • These results feed directly into the fracture tolerance strategy and testing plan for future IDE submissions.
  • Set next steps: repeat tensile pull testing on samples soaked in saline to capture clinically relevant conditions, and formalize the test method for design verification.
